China's passenger-car market shrank sharply in August. The China Passenger Car Association (CPCA) counted 1.541 million domestic passenger-car retail sales, down 23.6% from a year earlier. Chinese brands did not escape: their retail sales fell 19%. But the market fell faster for everyone else, so their share of domestic retail rose to 69.9%, up 4.1 percentage points. Share went up. Volume went down. Those are different things, and the gap between them explains most of this month's headlines.

## Share up, volume down

The CPCA's breakdown shows where the pain landed. Mainstream joint-venture brands sold about 310,000 cars at retail, down 35%. German brands' share fell 2.1 points to 12.5%, and Japanese brands' share fell 1.4 points to 10.9%. Fuel-powered cars as a whole fell about 40%: Chinese-brand petrol cars were down 45% and joint-venture ones down 40%. Luxury brands held up best, with retail down 26% and share off only 0.3 points.

The CPCA ties the petrol collapse to higher fuel prices. New-energy vehicles reached 65.2% of retail sales, a record, even though their own volume fell 10.1%. Fuel cars shrank faster than electric ones, so electrification rose. We should be careful about what this proves. Chinese brands lost less than their rivals. It does not show which customer moved from which brand to which.

## The gap is filled abroad

If Chinese brands sold 19% fewer cars at home, how did their factory volumes grow? Exports. The CPCA reports passenger-car exports, including kits for local assembly, of 888,000 in August, up 77.8%. That is 38% of manufacturers' passenger-car volume, against 20% a year earlier. Chinese brands account for 780,000 of those cars, up 82%. Their wholesale, which counts factory shipments including exports, rose 5% while their domestic retail fell 19%.

So a carmaker's headline monthly total is not a domestic number. It includes a large and fast-growing export share, and it cannot be set against the market's 23.6% fall. The CPCA's own reading is that exports are propping up manufacturers' wholesale and capacity.

## BYD and Geely: two different scoreboards

BYD sold 440,293 new-energy vehicles in August, up 17.84%, according to its exchange filing. Geely sold 270,194 cars, up 8%. Both totals include exports.

The CPCA data lets us split the month, for new-energy cars only. BYD's domestic new-energy retail was 233,943 cars, about 15% of the whole passenger-car market by our arithmetic. Its new-energy exports were 184,446, against new-energy wholesale of 432,684: roughly two in every five cars. Geely's domestic new-energy retail was 110,560. Its new-energy exports were 69,910, against wholesale of 173,675: again about two in five. Geely's petrol cars are not in these figures, so they are not a full-brand share.

We can say less about domestic share. Press reports suggest Geely's share of retail rose on last year and BYD's was roughly flat. We have not been able to confirm either against the CPCA's own manufacturer table, so we are not quoting figures. If both are right, BYD's domestic sales fell roughly in line with the market, and its growth came from abroad.

BYD's breakdown shows where its strength lies. Battery-electric sales rose 28.38% to 256,230. Plug-in hybrids, the backbone of its domestic volume, crept up 3.05%. Year to date, NEV sales of 2.67 million are still down 6.84%, with plug-in hybrids down 11.22%. August was a good month in a bad year. We traced the start of that slide in [BYD's Sales Slump Deepens in Early 2026](/blog/byd-sales-decline-early-2026).

Inside Geely the picture splits. Zeekr sold 36,981 cars, up 110%, while Lynk & Co fell 37%. Same group, opposite directions.

## New entrants are gaining in electric

Within new-energy retail, the CPCA says Chinese brands as a group held 63.7%, down 6.1 points. New-force makers, led by Leapmotor and NIO in the CPCA's account, held 26.0%, up 5.5 points. The CPCA separately counts the independent new-energy brands of traditional groups at 15.1%, up 2.3 points. The categories are defined differently and overlap, so this is not a clean transfer from one company to another. It does show the electric segment reshuffling while the whole market shrinks.

## Survival now has a number

Changan chairman Zhu Huarong said this week, according to Chinese press reports, that over the next three to five years 3.5 million cars a year is the volume at which a carmaker can "only just survive". Not thrive. Survive.

Few Chinese groups clear that bar. Geely sold 1.94 million cars in the first eight months, which annualises to roughly 2.9 million. In August, Li Auto delivered 37,679 cars, XPeng 39,107 and NIO 35,836. Together the three came to about a quarter of BYD's August volume. Each has a technology story. None has a scale story.

Chinese press also reports that the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) said carmakers' efforts to cut their dependence on battery maker CATL, known as "de-CATL", should stop. We have seen this only in secondary reports and have not found the ministry's own wording, so treat it as unconfirmed.

## Exports are the pressure valve, and it may narrow

Exports are holding up manufacturers' volumes. One industry projection puts China's total auto exports at a record 10 million units this year. Press reports point to two pressures on that.

The first is fiscal. Citi, citing a CPCA expert, expects China's export tax rebate on new-energy vehicles to be phased down in stages.

The second is European. Automotive News and Electric-Vehicles.com report that Beijing has rejected a voluntary cap on plug-in hybrid exports to the EU and urged European industry bodies, ACEA and VDA, to oppose new restrictions. Plug-in hybrids have been surging into Europe, and they sit outside the EU's 2024 countervailing duties, which target battery-electric cars. Citi reads the EU's share cap as a bargaining chip, so the outcome is not settled.

The next year will sort China's carmakers into two groups: those that can sell abroad at a profit without a rebate, and those selling abroad because they can no longer sell at home. August's numbers cannot yet tell the two apart. The September export data, and whatever Brussels decides on hybrids, will.
